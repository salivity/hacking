How Voltage Glitching Skips CPU Authentication

Voltage glitching is a hardware fault-injection technique where an attacker briefly drops the power supply rail of a microcontroller or microprocessor to induce deterministic behavioral errors. By creating an electrical transient precisely when the target executes an authentication routine, the attacker causes setup time violations in the processor's digital logic. This article explains the underlying physics of propagation delay, how transient undervoltage corrupts instruction execution within a clock cycle, and how attackers leverage this phenomenon to skip critical conditional branch instructions that protect secure hardware.

Digital Logic, Propagation Delay, and Clock Timing

Synchronous digital processors rely on clock signals to coordinate data movement between flip-flops and logic gates. During each clock cycle, signals travel from a source register, pass through combinational logic (such as an Arithmetic Logic Unit), and arrive at a destination register.

For the destination register to reliably store the correct logic state, the electrical signal must reach the register input and remain stable for a minimum duration before the active clock edge arrives. This requirement is known as setup time (\(t_{setup}\)). The total time required for a signal to transition through the combinational path is the propagation delay (\(t_{pd}\)). The system maintains timing closure only when:

\[T_{clock} \ge t_{pd} + t_{setup}\]

A transistor's switching speed depends directly on the supplied core voltage (\(V_{dd}\)). When \(V_{dd}\) remains within manufacturer specifications, internal MOSFETs charge and discharge parasitic capacitances quickly enough to meet setup time requirements.

How Voltage Glitching Induces Setup Time Violations

Voltage fault injection exploits the relationship between supply voltage and propagation delay. An attacker connects a high-speed switching circuit (typically an N-channel MOSFET configured as a "crowbar") between the target chip's core power rail and ground.

When the attacker triggers the crowbar circuit for several nanoseconds, the core voltage drops precipitously below the minimum operational threshold:

  1. Increased Propagation Delay: The reduced voltage starves the internal transistors of current, severely slowing down gate switching speeds and dramatically increasing \(t_{pd}\).
  2. Clock Desynchronization: While the internal logic slows down, the external or internal clock continues to oscillate at its standard frequency (\(T_{clock}\)).
  3. Setup Time Violations: Signals traversing the combinational logic fail to reach the destination flip-flops before the clock latches the data.
  4. Metastability and Corrupted State: The destination registers latch incomplete or transient voltage levels, resolving unpredictably to logical high (1) or low (0) instead of the true computed result.

From Electrical Fault to Instruction Skipping

Modern embedded processors process instructions through a pipelined architecture consisting of Fetch, Decode, Execute, and Writeback stages. A voltage glitch can disrupt this pipeline in several deterministic ways:

  • Corrupting the Opcode Fetch: If the voltage drops while an instruction is being transferred from Flash or SRAM into the instruction register, the data bus lines may fail to charge completely. The processor decodes the corrupted bit pattern into a different instruction—frequently an undefined instruction that decodes as a NOP (No Operation), or an instruction that modifies unrelated registers.
  • Corrupting Operand Evaluation: If the glitch occurs during the execute phase, the comparator logic inside the ALU fails to complete its calculation, leaving status flags (such as the Zero, Carry, or Negative flags) in an unmodified or incorrect state.
  • Program Counter Corruption: A glitch can disrupt the increment logic or write logic of the Program Counter (PC), causing the processor to skip past instruction words entirely.

Bypassing Authentication Routines

At the assembly level, authentication routines rely on conditional branch instructions to grant or deny access. Consider a simplified firmware authentication check:

cmp r0, r1      ; Compare entered key (r0) with master key (r1)
beq authenticated ; Branch to unlock routine if keys match
b   lockout       ; Fall through: keys do not match, lock device

In a secure system, entering an invalid key causes the comparison to clear the Zero Flag (\(Z = 0\)). Consequently, the beq (Branch if Equal) instruction evaluates as false, and execution continues to the lockout routine.

An attacker synchronizes the voltage glitch with the execution of either the cmp or the beq instruction using a hardware trigger (such as a rising edge on a serial TX line, a GPIO toggle, or an exact power consumption profile).

If the glitch hits the cmp instruction, the ALU fails to clear the Zero Flag, potentially leaving a prior \(Z = 1\) state intact. If the glitch hits the beq instruction, the instruction decoder misreads the opcode bits. Because many ARM and RISC-V architectures treat corrupted or zeroed instruction encodings as a NOP, the CPU simply does nothing during that clock cycle and advances the Program Counter.

Instead of jumping to the lockout handler, the CPU falls through the conditional branch or lands directly into the authenticated execution path, granting the attacker access to privileged firmware, debug shells, or decryption keys without presenting valid credentials.

Maritime AIS Vulnerabilities and Vessel Spoofing

Maritime Automatic Identification Systems (AIS) are critical for global vessel tracking and collision avoidance, yet they fundamentally lack cryptographic security. This article examines the core vulnerabilities within the AIS protocol that allow threat actors to manufacture and broadcast non-existent vessels—commonly referred to as "ghost ships." By analyzing the absence of message authentication, the widespread accessibility of software-defined radio technology, and the structural limitations of coastal and satellite transponders, this overview details how attackers manipulate maritime domain awareness systems and explores the operational consequences of these security flaws.

Lack of Authentication and Encryption

The primary vulnerability within the AIS specification (ITU-R M.1371) is the complete absence of encryption and digital signatures. Designed in the 1990s as an open, cooperative broadcast system using very high frequency (VHF) maritime channels (161.975 MHz and 162.025 MHz), AIS prioritizes universal interoperability and message deliverability over data integrity.

Because messages are transmitted in cleartext without an authentication handshake, receiving transponders, coastal tracking stations, and satellite constellations have no native cryptographic mechanism to verify whether a transmission originates from a physical vessel or an adversary.

Accessibility via Software-Defined Radios

Generating valid AIS packets historically required specialized marine hardware. Modern software-defined radios (SDRs)—such as the HackRF One, BladeRF, or USRP—have lowered this technical barrier. Combined with open-source software libraries, an attacker can construct, encode, and transmit arbitrary AIS AIVDM/AIVDO sentences from a standard computer.

Using these tools, a threat actor can craft the specific bitstream required by Gaussian Minimum Shift Keying (GMSK) modulation used in AIS:

  • MMSI Generation: Generating an arbitrary, non-assigned, or spoofed Maritime Mobile Service Identity (MMSI) number.
  • Kinematic Data Ingestion: Embedding arbitrary Global Positioning System (GPS) coordinates, speed over ground (SOG), course over ground (COG), and heading into AIS Message Types 1, 2, or 3.
  • Static and Voyage Data: Broadcasting Message Type 5 packets containing fabricated ship names, call signs, IMO numbers, ship types, and destinations.

Exploitation of Time-Division Multiple Access

AIS coordinates transmissions across vessels using Self-Organizing Time-Division Multiple Access (SOTDMA) or Carrier-Sense Time-Division Multiple Access (CSTDMA). In SOTDMA, transponders synchronize with UTC time slots to announce when they will transmit next, ensuring minimal message collision across local radio coverage.

Attackers can exploit this architecture by synchronizing an SDR transmission to standard GPS time. Once synchronized, the rogue transmitter claims time slots, injecting fake vessel profiles into the local RF environment without disrupting the physical synchronization of the network. Receiving vessels automatically register these time slots as genuine targets.

Impact on Navigational Systems

When spoofed transmissions enter the RF spectrum, onboard and shore-side navigation tools accept the data at face value:

  • ECDIS Integration: Electronic Chart Display and Information Systems (ECDIS) plot the spoofed vessel directly on navigation screens alongside legitimate radar and chart overlays.
  • False Collision Warnings: Spoofed vessels programmed on a collision course with real ships trigger automated Distance to Closest Point of Approach (DCPA) and Time to Closest Point of Approach (TCPA) alarms, potentially forcing evasive maneuvers in restricted waterways.
  • Ghost Fleets and Geofence Manipulation: Coordinated transmission of dozens of fake vessels can obscure legitimate maritime traffic, mask covert operations, or trigger automated environmental and territorial defense alerts.

Limitations in Current Defenses

Remediating these vulnerabilities is complicated by legacy infrastructure and international standardization processes. Proposals for Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) or cryptographic authentication face bandwidth limitations on narrowband VHF channels and require global retrofitting of thousands of vessels.

Current mitigation relies almost exclusively on multi-sensor data fusion—cross-referencing incoming AIS targets with primary marine radar, satellite synthetic aperture radar (SAR), and optical tracking—to identify targets that exist as radio signals without physical presence on the water.

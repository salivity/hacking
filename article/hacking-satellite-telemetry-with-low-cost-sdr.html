Hacking Satellite Telemetry With Low-Cost SDR

Capturing and decoding unencrypted telemetry from orbiting satellites no longer requires enterprise-grade ground stations or classified military hardware. Using inexpensive Software Defined Radio (SDR) USB dongles paired with DIY antennas and open-source software, security researchers and radio enthusiasts can intercept real-time downlinks from space. This article explores the hardware, software pipelines, and radio frequency (RF) mechanics required to intercept, demodulate, and parse satellite health, orbital telemetry, and scientific payloads directly from orbit.

The Low-Cost Hardware Stack

The foundation of modern satellite eavesdropping is the RTL-SDR, a repurposed DVB-T TV tuner based on the Realtek RTL2832U chip that costs between $25 and $40. These devices operate as wideband radio receivers capable of tuning across frequencies typically ranging from 24 MHz to 1.7 GHz—a spectrum that covers most low Earth orbit (LEO) satellite downlinks.

Antenna design must match the target frequency and polarization. Because signals from orbiting satellites twist as they travel through the ionosphere (Faraday rotation), circularly polarized antennas are preferred. Common low-cost builds include:

  • V-Dipole Antennas: Tuned specifically for 137 MHz weather satellites (such as NOAA-15, 18, and 19).
  • Quadrifilar Helix (QFH) or Turnstile Antennas: Ideal for omnidirectional reception of circularly polarized signals across the VHF and UHF bands.
  • Yagi-Uda Antennas: Handheld directional antennas used for higher-gain tracking of weak CubeSat signals in the 435–438 MHz amateur satellite band.

Tracking and Doppler Correction

Satellites in low Earth orbit travel at roughly 7.5 kilometers per second, meaning a line-of-sight pass lasts only 5 to 15 minutes. Intercepting the signal requires knowing exact pass times and adjusting for the Doppler effect, which compresses the frequency as the satellite approaches and stretches it as it moves away.

Operators use open-source satellite tracking software such as Gpredict or Orbitron, driven by Two-Line Element sets (TLEs) published regularly by organizations like Celestrak. These tracking programs interface directly with SDR software via network protocols to dynamically adjust the receiver’s center frequency in real time, neutralizing Doppler shift automatically.

Intercepting the Raw RF Signal

The antenna routes the physical radio wave into the SDR, which samples the analog RF spectrum and converts it into digital I/Q (In-phase and Quadrature) data streams. General-purpose SDR control software—such as SDR++, GQRX, or SDR#—visualizes the spectrum via an interactive fast Fourier transform (FFT) waterfall display.

When the target satellite rises above the horizon, its transmission appears as a distinct spike or pattern on the waterfall. The SDR software applies bandpass filtering to isolate the transmission from adjacent noise and records the raw audio or baseband I/Q file to disk.

Demodulating and Decoding Telemetry

Raw I/Q data represents the physical modulation scheme used by the satellite, typically Frequency Shift Keying (FSK), Audio Frequency Shift Keying (AFSK), or Phase Shift Keying (BPSK). Translating this waveform into readable digital data requires a dedicated demodulator and protocol decoder.

  1. Demodulation: Software suites like GNU Radio or dedicated utilities like direwolf and soundmodem process the audio/baseband signal, stripping the carrier frequency and recovering the raw bitstream.
  2. Framing: The bitstream is framed into standardized packet formats. Many amateur satellites and educational CubeSats utilize the AX.25 packet radio protocol or CCSDS (Consultative Committee for Space Data Systems) standards.
  3. Parsing: Multi-mission decoders like SatDump, or satellite-specific telemetry dashboards, ingest the framed packets. The software maps raw hex values against published telemetry definitions to display real-time engineering metrics: solar panel voltage, battery charge levels, onboard temperatures, spin rates, and operational subsystem statuses.

Security Implications

A substantial number of scientific satellites, amateur CubeSats, and legacy orbital assets transmit telemetry without cryptographic protection or authentication. While passively listening to downlinks is generally legal and non-disruptive, analyzing this unencrypted telemetry exposes the internal state of the spacecraft. In security research, understanding these open telemetry streams is frequently the first reconnaissance step in identifying weak uplink-verification schemes, predictable command structures, or flaws in ground-to-space communication architectures.

Rogue BGP Routing Techniques in Nation-State Cyberattacks

Border Gateway Protocol (BGP) governs how data traverses the global internet by establishing routes between Autonomous Systems (ASes). Because BGP was fundamentally designed around an implicit trust model rather than cryptographic verification, sophisticated nation-state actors frequently exploit it to divert, intercept, and inspect sensitive communications. This article outlines the primary techniques threat actors use to inject rogue BGP announcements, reroute international network paths through compromised infrastructure, and maintain stealth while executing large-scale surveillance operations.

More-Specific Prefix Announcements (Longest Prefix Match)

The most reliable method for diverting traffic relies on the fundamental routing rule known as the Longest Prefix Match. In BGP, routers always prioritize more specific network prefixes over broader ones. If a legitimate organization advertises an IP block as a /21 network, a malicious actor can announce two /22 subnets or multiple /24 subnets covering the exact same IP space.

Because the rogue announcements are more granular, routers globally select the malicious path as the optimal route, steering traffic away from the legitimate target and directly into the attacker's infrastructure.

AS-Path Forgery and Prepending

BGP includes a built-in loop prevention mechanism: routers discard any routing update that already contains their own Autonomous System Number (ASN) in the AS-PATH attribute. Threat actors subvert this mechanism and bypass rudimentary upstream route filters by forging the AS-PATH.

In an AS-Path spoofing attack, the actor artificially appends the victim's ASN to the end of the advertised path, or prepends trusted transit ASNs to simulate a legitimate multihomed relationship. This makes the rogue announcement appear authentic to peering partners while preventing the real owner from easily adopting the bad route, helping the attacker selectively control which geographic regions adopt the forged path.

Traffic Redirection and Man-in-the-Middle (MITM)

Directly blackholing traffic causes immediate service outages that trigger incident alerts. Nation-state intelligence campaigns instead prioritize long-term surveillance through BGP-based Man-in-the-Middle (MITM) attacks.

In this scenario, the actor announces a rogue route to capture inbound traffic, routes it through an inspection infrastructure equipped with passive collectors or SSL/TLS decryption tools, and simultaneously maintains an unpoisoned upstream tunnel to forward the packets to the legitimate destination. Because latency increases only marginally and traffic reaches its target, the interception can operate undetected for hours or even days.

Deliberate Route Leaking

A route leak occurs when an Autonomous System propagates a routing announcement received from one provider or peer to another provider or peer in violation of standard peering agreements. Nation-state actors often leverage state-controlled Internet Service Providers (ISPs) or compromised regional telecoms to deliberately leak internal transit routes to Tier-1 international carriers.

When a major transit provider accepts and redistributes these leaked routes, massive volumes of international traffic are pulled across foreign borders and through state-monitored internet exchange points (IXPs) before returning to standard global backbones.

Compromising Core Routing Infrastructure

Rather than attempting to spoof routes from external networks that enforce strict validation, advanced actors compromise edge routers belonging to legitimate transit providers, Tier-2 networks, or national telecommunications companies. By gaining administrative access via stolen credentials, supply-chain vulnerabilities, or unpatched network hardware, attackers can alter BGP configurations natively on trusted hardware. Announcements generated from compromised, reputable ASNs effortlessly bypass basic prefix filters and neighboring peer reputation checks, ensuring wide global propagation.

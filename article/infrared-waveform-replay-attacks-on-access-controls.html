Infrared Waveform Replay Attacks on Access Controls

Infrared (IR) waveform replay attacks exploit legacy physical security mechanisms by capturing and re-transmitting optical control signals. While modern physical access systems predominantly utilize encrypted radio frequencies (RF) or wired credentials, certain automated shutters, industrial barriers, and legacy perimeter controls still rely on direct line-of-sight infrared transmitters. This article examines the mechanics behind capturing IR signals, the vulnerabilities inherent in static-code implementations, and the technical countermeasures required to mitigate unauthorized physical entry.

Mechanics of Infrared Signal Transmission

Infrared control systems transmit binary data using pulsed light, typically in the 850 nm to 950 nm wavelength spectrum. Data is encoded onto a carrier frequency—commonly between 36 kHz and 40 kHz—to distinguish the signal from ambient ambient light and optical noise.

Standard IR protocols (such as NEC, RC-5, or proprietary pulse-distance formats) represent binary values through variations in pulse duration or intervals between pulses:

  • Carrier Modulation: The emitter rapidly pulses the IR LED at the carrier frequency.
  • Pulse Distance/Width Modulation: A "0" or "1" is determined by the length of the burst or the length of the space following a burst.
  • Command Packets: A standard frame includes a preamble (start burst), device address, command byte, and often an inverted check byte to detect transmission errors.

The Replay Vulnerability in Physical Access Controls

The fundamental weakness that enables bypass via recorded IR signals is the use of static, unencrypted commands. Many legacy barriers and commercial roll-up shutters utilize simple receivers paired with single-button transmitters that emit identical data frames each time they are actuated.

  1. Signal Interception: An optical sensor tuned to standard carrier frequencies can record the raw light sequence without needing to decode the underlying protocol. Because IR requires line-of-sight, this capture can occur from significant distances if high-sensitivity photodiodes or optical lenses are used to direct incoming light.
  2. Digital Reconstruction: The captured signal is stored as a series of microsecond-level time intervals denoting carrier "on" and "off" states.
  3. Transmission: A microcontroller connected to high-output IR LEDs reproduces the exact timing intervals.
  4. Execution: The receiver detects the expected preamble and command sequence, validating the instruction as if it originated from an authorized physical remote, triggering the relay that opens the barrier or shutter.

Because the receiver does not check for freshness, sequence numbers, or cryptographic signatures, any captured signal within range can be repeatedly applied to manipulate the physical barrier.

Limitations of Infrared Attacks

While technically straightforward against static systems, infrared-based physical attacks face strict operational constraints:

  • Line of Sight: Infrared light does not penetrate opaque structures, walls, or heavy glass coated with anti-reflective/UV-blocking films. The interception and playback must have a direct or reflective optical path to the receiver.
  • Environmental Interference: High-intensity ambient light (such as direct sunlight or incandescent factory lighting) can saturate photodiodes, leading to transmission dropouts or inaccurate recording.
  • Range Constraints: Standard high-power consumer LEDs typically operate within ranges of 10 to 30 meters, requiring proximity unless specialized optical arrays are deployed.

Defensive Remediation and Mitigations

To neutralize the risk of optical signal capture and playback, physical access infrastructure should implement cryptographic verification and robust architectural designs:

  • Rolling Codes (Hopping Codes): Systems should utilize pseudorandom code generators (such as KeeLoq or similar dynamic code algorithms). In a rolling code implementation, each transmission includes an encrypted counter value that changes with every button press. The receiver only accepts a command if the counter matches the expected cryptographic sequence, rendering recorded static transmissions useless.
  • Transition to Encrypted RF or RFID: Critical physical barriers should avoid basic IR controls in favor of bidirectional, cryptographically authenticated protocols (such as WPA-Enterprise wireless systems, MIFARE DESFire, or dynamic UHF RFID).
  • Supervised Wired Interfaces: Keypads, credential readers, and access controllers should communicate via supervised serial channels (such as OSDP with Secure Channel Protocol) rather than unauthenticated wireless or optical remotes.
  • Receiver Occlusion and Access Segregation: Physical receivers should be positioned where they cannot be triggered from public or uncontrolled access zones, minimizing the line-of-sight exposure required for external signal capture.

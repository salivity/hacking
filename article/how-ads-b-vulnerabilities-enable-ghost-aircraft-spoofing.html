How ADS-B Vulnerabilities Enable Ghost Aircraft Spoofing

Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast (ADS-B) serves as the primary surveillance technology for modern air traffic management, yet it was designed without native cryptographic authentication or encryption. This fundamental architectural flaw allows adversaries equipped with commercial software-defined radios (SDRs) to broadcast falsified radio frequency signals directly to ground stations and nearby aircraft. By transmitting synthetic telemetry data that systems accept as legitimate, attackers can generate non-existent "ghost aircraft," deceiving air traffic controllers and airborne collision avoidance systems.

The Architectural Flaw: Open and Unauthenticated Signals

ADS-B relies on Mode S Extended Squitter pulses transmitted primarily on the 1090 MHz radio frequency (and 978 MHz Universal Access Transceiver in low-altitude US airspace). When designed, the protocol prioritized interoperability, simplicity, and low latency over cybersecurity.

Consequently, ADS-B messages possess three major security vulnerabilities:

  • No Authentication: Transmissions do not include digital signatures, Message Authentication Codes (MACs), or public-key cryptographic proofs to verify the sender's identity.
  • No Encryption: Messages broadcast in cleartext, revealing aircraft ID (ICAO 24-bit address), call sign, position, velocity, and altitude to anyone within radio line-of-sight.
  • No Integrity Checking Beyond CRC: The standard Cyclic Redundancy Check (CRC) detects accidental transmission errors and bit-flips, but it provides zero protection against malicious modification or intentional packet creation, as an attacker can simply compute a valid CRC for any forged payload.

The Mechanics of Injecting Ghost Aircraft

Creating ghost aircraft requires minimal specialized equipment. Using readily available, low-cost Software-Defined Radios (such as a HackRF, BladeRF, or USRP) combined with open-source modulation software, an attacker can execute message injection attacks in real time:

  1. Packet Crafting: The adversary constructs valid ADS-B message frames containing arbitrary data, assigning a spoofed 24-bit ICAO address, realistic latitude and longitude coordinates, realistic flight vectors, and valid barometric altitudes.
  2. Pulse Position Modulation (PPM): The crafted binary data is encoded into pulses using standard PPM at a 1-megabit-per-second data rate.
  3. RF Transmission: The SDR upconverts the baseband signal to 1090 MHz and transmits it. If the transmission power matches or exceeds legitimate signals reaching local receivers, the receiving hardware accepts the transmission.

Because ADS-B receivers process any mathematically valid packet as authentic, the receiving equipment treats the crafted signal as a physical plane, populating it on tracking systems as a legitimate target.

Operational Impacts on Aviation Safety

The presence of unverified ghost aircraft degrades airspace operations in several ways:

  • TCAS Deception: The Traffic Collision Avoidance System (TCAS) installed on commercial airliners calculates collision threats using transponder data. A spoofed aircraft projected onto a head-on collision path can trigger Traffic Advisories (TA) or Resolution Advisories (RA), commanding the pilot to dive, climb, or adjust trajectory to avoid a non-existent threat.
  • Air Traffic Control Disruption: Controllers relying on NextGen radar displays may experience false radar tracks, leading to unnecessary delays, aborted landings, diversion of holding patterns, or increased workload during critical flight phases.
  • Denial of Service via Saturation: An attacker can flood an airspace sector with hundreds of simultaneous ghost trajectories, cluttering displays, exhausting tracking table memory in ground processors, and effectively blinding monitoring systems.

Challenges in Implementing Defenses

Solving the ADS-B spoofing vector is non-trivial due to legacy constraints. Introducing digital signatures is constrained by the narrow 112-bit message limit of standard ADS-B frames, where extra authentication payloads would consume vital bandwidth and cause high packet collision rates on 1090 MHz. Furthermore, establishing a global Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) for hundreds of thousands of civil and military aircraft across multiple international jurisdictions presents major operational hurdles.

To counter ghost aircraft without altering the core standard, aviation authorities increasingly deploy secondary verification techniques. These include Multilateration (MLAT)—which validates an aircraft's physical location by calculating the Time Difference of Arrival (TDOA) of its radio signals across multiple ground sensors—and cross-verification against primary surveillance radar (PSR), which relies on physical reflections rather than unauthenticated broadcast data.

Predicting Linear Congruential Generators in Gaming

This article examines the mathematical vulnerabilities of linear congruential generators (LCGs), explaining how their internal states can be determined from observed outputs and why this predictability historically exposed non-secure gambling systems to exploitation. By understanding the linear relationships underlying LCGs, one can see how attackers mathematically reconstruct the state to forecast future outcomes, and why regulated gaming environments mandate cryptographically secure alternatives.

The Mechanics of Linear Congruential Generators

A linear congruential generator is one of the oldest and simplest pseudorandom number generator (PRNG) algorithms. It produces a sequence of numbers using a linear recurrence relation defined by the formula:

\[X_{n+1} = (a \cdot X_n + c) \pmod m\]

Where:

  • \(X\) is the sequence of pseudorandom values.
  • \(m\) is the modulus (\(m > 0\)).
  • \(a\) is the multiplier (\(0 < a < m\)).
  • \(c\) is the increment (\(0 \le c < m\)).
  • \(X_0\) is the starting value, known as the seed.

Because the formula relies entirely on modular arithmetic, the next state (\(X_{n+1}\)) is strictly determined by the current state (\(X_n\)). If the generator's state is exposed, all subsequent numbers in the sequence can be calculated instantly.

State Reconstruction from Known Parameters

In systems where the parameters (\(a\), \(c\), and \(m\)) are standardized or extracted from client software or firmware, predicting the sequence is trivial.

  1. Full State Output: If the generator directly outputs the entire value of \(X_n\), observing a single output reveals the entire current state. The observer calculates \(X_{n+1} = (a \cdot X_n + c) \pmod m\) to determine the exact outcome of the next roll, spin, or card dealt.
  2. Hidden Increments: If the increment \(c\) is unknown but \(a\) and \(m\) are known, observing two consecutive states (\(X_0, X_1\)) allows an attacker to compute \(c\) directly via modular subtraction: \[c = (X_1 - a \cdot X_0) \pmod m\]

Recovering Unknown Parameters

Even if the parameters (\(a, c, m\)) are kept secret, they can be determined through modular cryptanalysis using a small sequence of observed full outputs.

By taking the differences between consecutive states, the additive constant \(c\) is eliminated: \[D_n = X_{n+1} - X_n\] \[D_{n+1} \equiv a \cdot D_n \pmod m\]

With enough consecutive values, the multiplier \(a\) and the modulus \(m\) can be recovered by computing the greatest common divisor (GCD) of determinants derived from these difference equations. Once \(m\) and \(a\) are recovered, \(c\) is calculated, completely exposing the generator.

The Truncated LCG Problem

In practice, gambling systems rarely output the raw internal state \(X_n\). Instead, they scale the number down or discard the least significant bits—for example, using modular reduction to select a number between 1 and 6 for a die roll or 0 to 36 for a roulette wheel. This is known as a truncated LCG.

While truncation obscures the full state, it does not provide cryptographic security:

  • Lattice Reduction Attacks: Truncated LCGs can be modeled as finding a short vector in a mathematical lattice. Using the Lenstra–Lenstra–Lovász (LLL) lattice basis reduction algorithm, analysts can solve for the hidden bits of the state using only a modest sequence of truncated outputs.
  • Timing and Physical Correlation: In older electronic gaming machines that used internal timers to re-seed or advance weak PRNGs, attackers recorded spin sequences over time to synchronize an external device with the machine's clock, allowing them to predict the precise fraction of a second to initiate a spin for favorable outcomes.

Defensive Standards and Mitigation

Due to these mathematical weaknesses, general-purpose PRNGs like LCGs, Mersenne Twister, and simple shift-register generators are fundamentally unsuitable for systems where unpredictability has economic value. Modern regulated gaming machines and online platforms mitigate state prediction attacks through strict compliance requirements:

  • Cryptographically Secure PRNGs (CSPRNGs): Modern systems use algorithms designed to resist state-reconstruction attacks (such as AES-CTR-based DRBGs or ChaCha20), ensuring that past outputs cannot be used to infer future states in polynomial time.
  • Hardware Entropy (TRNGs): True Random Number Generators derive entropy from unpredictable physical phenomena (such as thermal noise or quantum fluctuations) to continuously seed and re-seed the generator.
  • Independent Auditing: Regulatory bodies require rigorous testing (e.g., NIST SP 800-22 suites and dieharder tests) to verify that game outcomes exhibit statistical randomness and that internal states cannot be reconstructed from external play.

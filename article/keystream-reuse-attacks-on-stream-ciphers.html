Keystream Reuse Attacks on Stream Ciphers

Keystream reuse—often referred to as the "two-time pad" attack—is one of the most devastating cryptographic implementation flaws, reducing unbreakable or strong encryption into easily decipherable text. Both One-Time Pads (OTPs) and stream ciphers (such as ChaCha20 or RC4) generate a stream of pseudorandom bits and combine them with plaintext using the bitwise exclusive-OR (XOR) operation. When an identical keystream is used to encrypt two or more different plaintexts, the shared key material mathematically cancels out, leaving the plaintexts exposed to trivial extraction through statistical analysis and crib dragging.

The Underlying Mathematics

Stream ciphers and one-time pads encrypt plaintext \(P\) into ciphertext \(C\) using a keystream \(K\):

\[C_1 = P_1 \oplus K\]

\[C_2 = P_2 \oplus K\]

When an attacker intercepts two ciphertexts encrypted with the same keystream, they can XOR the two ciphertexts together:

\[C_1 \oplus C_2 = (P_1 \oplus K) \oplus (P_2 \oplus K)\]

Because the XOR operation is associative, commutative, and self-inverting (\(K \oplus K = 0\)), the keystream completely vanishes:

\[C_1 \oplus C_2 = P_1 \oplus P_2\]

The attacker is no longer facing a cryptographic puzzle involving a secret key; instead, they have obtained the direct XOR sum of the two original messages.

Exploitation via Crib Dragging

Once an attacker possesses \(P_1 \oplus P_2\), recovering both messages relies on structural redundancies in human language or standard data formats. The primary method for doing this is "crib dragging."

A "crib" is a guessed word or phrase expected to appear in one of the plaintexts (such as "HTTP/1.1", "Bearer ", "{ \"status\":", or simply common English words like " the ").

The attack proceeds systematically:

  1. The attacker assumes the crib occurs at byte offset \(i\) in \(P_1\).
  2. They XOR the crib with \(C_1 \oplus C_2\) across that specific offset.
  3. If the guess is correct, the output will reveal valid, readable plaintext from message \(P_2\) at offset \(i\).
  4. If the output yields unreadable control characters or nonsensical text, the crib is shifted by one byte, and the process repeats.

Once partial text in \(P_2\) is revealed, the attacker can deduce surrounding words in \(P_2\) by context, turn those newly discovered words into cribs, and apply them back to reveal more of \(P_1\). This alternating process continues until both plaintexts are fully reconstructed.

Exploiting Natural Language Redundancies

Even without a specific crib, natural language characteristics make automated recovery straightforward:

  • The Space Character Property: In standard ASCII, the space character (byte value 0x20) XORed with any alphabetic character simply toggles the case of that letter. Because spaces are the most frequent character in English text, XOR collisions between spaces and letters reliably expose character values.
  • Grammar and Frequency Models: Attackers use n-gram frequency models to score candidate byte sequences automatically. If a key is reused across three or more ciphertexts (\(C_1, C_2, C_3, \dots, C_n\)), statistical convergence happens rapidly, allowing automated scripts to recover the entire plaintext in seconds without manual intervention.

Prevention

Stream ciphers require unique initialization vectors (IVs) or nonces for every encryption operation performed under the same key. A single repetition of a (Key, Nonce) tuple invalidates the confidentiality guarantees of the cipher. Secure systems ensure nonces are either strictly incremented sequential counters or sufficiently long random values (e.g., 96 or 192 bits) where collision probability is negligible over the system's operational lifetime.

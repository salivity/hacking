Physics Behind Electromagnetic Fault Injection

Electromagnetic fault injection (EMFI) is a non-invasive hardware hacking technique that disrupts a microchip's internal state to bypass security checks, skip critical instructions, or extract cryptographic keys. By generating brief, powerful, and localized electromagnetic fields, an attacker induces unintended electrical anomalies within the target silicon. This article explores the fundamental physics principles—predominantly Faraday’s law of induction, semiconductor carrier dynamics, and transmission timing alterations—that govern how magnetic pulses translate into exploitable digital faults.

Faraday’s Law of Induction and Transient Magnetic Fields

At the core of EMFI lies Faraday’s Law of Electromagnetic Induction. The injection hardware consists of a high-voltage pulse generator discharged through a microscopic inductive coil placed micrometers above the target integrated circuit (IC).

When a sharp current pulse (\(di/dt\)) discharges through the probe's coil, it creates a rapidly changing local magnetic flux density (\(dB/dt\)). According to Faraday's law, a time-varying magnetic field induces an electromotive force (EMF) in any closed conductive loop within its proximity:

\[\mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}\]

Inside an IC, metal interconnect layers, bond wires, and power distribution networks act as conductive loops. The transient magnetic field penetrates the chip's packaging and couples into these micro-traces, generating unexpected voltage and current spikes that ride on top of the chip’s regular operational signals.

Induced Voltages and Logic Level Corruption

Digital CMOS logic interprets logic states ('0' or '1') based on defined voltage thresholds relative to supply rails (\(V_{DD}\) and ground \(V_{SS}\)). The induced EMF generated by the EM pulse shifts the instantaneous voltage along the interconnects:

  • Constructive Interference: If the induced voltage is positive and coincides with a logic low, the total voltage can surpass the input threshold of a downstream gate, causing the gate to register an erroneous logic high.
  • Destructive Interference: If the induced voltage is negative during a logic high, it pulls the signal below the recognition threshold, causing a temporary logic low.

Because modern microcontrollers operate at low voltages (often between 1.0V and 3.3V) with tight noise margins, a modest induced spike of a few hundred millivolts is sufficient to corrupt a data bus or control line, creating bit flips.

Semiconductor Carrier Dynamics and Parasitic Effects

The physics of EMFI also extends to the silicon substrate itself. An intense, localized electromagnetic field interacts with the charge carriers (electrons and holes) within the doped semiconductor regions:

  1. Transient Electric Fields in the Substrate: The electric field component of the EM pulse exerts a Lorentz force on free carriers, causing drift currents within the substrate.
  2. Parasitic Diode Conduction: Complementary MOS (CMOS) structures inherently contain parasitic bipolar junction transistors (BJTs) arranged in p-n-p-n configurations. Local voltage spikes caused by the EM pulse can forward-bias these normally reverse-biased p-n junctions, injecting unwanted minority carriers into sensitive transistor channels.
  3. Channel Inversion Disruption: If the localized field alters the electric potential across a MOSFET gate oxide, it modifies the channel conductance independently of the actual gate control signal, temporarily forcing a transistor into an open or closed state.

Propagation Delays and Setup-Time Violations

Rather than directly flipping static memory bits, EMFI is most commonly leveraged to induce transient timing violations during instruction execution.

A CMOS transistor's switching speed depends directly on its supply voltage:

\[I_{DS} \propto (V_{GS} - V_{TH})^2\]

When an EM pulse causes a momentary voltage drop across the internal power distribution grid, the drive current of affected logic gates plummets. This surge in gate propagation delay causes the output of a combinatorial logic circuit to arrive late at the destination flip-flop.

When the logic signal fails to stabilize before the next clock edge, a setup-time violation occurs. The register latches incorrect, metastable, or default zero states. At the machine-code level, this manifests as modifying an instruction opcode—such as altering a conditional branch check (BEQ/BNE) into a NOP (No Operation) or changing an authentication comparison result—allowing an attacker to bypass critical firmware security routines seamlessly.

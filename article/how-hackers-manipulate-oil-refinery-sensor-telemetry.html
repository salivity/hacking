How Hackers Manipulate Oil Refinery Sensor Telemetry

Industrial control systems within oil refineries rely on precise telemetry to balance temperature gradients across fractional distillation columns, ensuring proper hydrocarbon separation and structural safety. Cyber adversaries target this telemetry to induce thermal runaway, contaminate product streams, or blind operators to hazardous conditions. This article outlines the specific methods and technical vectors attackers use to manipulate temperature sensor data within refinery distillation environments.

Analog and Field-Level Tampering

Temperature transmitters in distillation columns typically utilize Resistance Temperature Detectors (RTDs) or thermocouples communicating via 4-20 mA current loops, frequently augmented by Highway Addressable Remote Transducer (HART) digital protocols. Attackers targeting the field network layer can exploit vulnerabilities in legacy field buses or unauthenticated HART networks. By connecting unauthorized field communicators or injecting malicious HART commands via compromised multi-drop networks, adversaries can recalibrate zero and span parameters, alter damping values, or freeze the transmitter output, causing the field device to report static, normal temperatures despite physical fluctuations inside the column.

Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) Protocol Injection

Once telemetry transitions from analog instrumentation to digital industrial protocols—such as Modbus TCP, Ethernet/IP, or proprietary Distributed Control System (DCS) protocols—it becomes vulnerable to network-based interception. Because many operational technology (OT) protocols lack native encryption or cryptographic message authentication, attackers positioning themselves within the Purdue Model’s Level 1 or Level 2 networks can perform Address Resolution Protocol (ARP) poisoning or packet injection. Attackers intercept raw analog-to-digital sensor values traveling between Remote Terminal Units (RTUs), Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs), and the DCS controllers, rewriting data packets in real time to present fabricated temperature gradients to automated safety systems.

Controller Logic and I/O Memory Manipulation

Adversaries with access to Level 2 engineering workstations can directly target the controller hardware managing the column. By altering the firmware or runtime logic of the PLC or safety instrumented system (SIS), attackers can overwrite the I/O image table in the controller’s memory. In this scenario, the sensor physically reads and transmits a correct higher or lower temperature, but modified control logic replaces the register value with a hardcoded, benign temperature reading before executing control routines. This technique effectively decouples automated reboiler steam valves and reflux flow adjustments from physical reality.

HMI Spoofing and Historical Telemetry Replay

Attackers frequently employ replay techniques similar to those observed in targeted industrial malware campaigns. By recording baseline temperature cycles during normal steady-state distillation operations, adversaries can sever real-time telemetry pipelines to the Human-Machine Interface (HMI) and loop the recorded benign data on operator dashboards. While operators observe stable temperatures within standard operating limits, the physical processes can be driven into over-pressurization, thermal degradation of catalyst trays, or boiling-point overlap without triggering manual intervention.

How LLL Algorithm Breaks Knapsack Cryptosystems

Knapsack-based cryptosystems rely on the computational hardness of the subset sum problem to encrypt data, but they can be systematically broken using lattice reduction techniques like the Lenstra–Lenstra–Lovász (LLL) algorithm. By mapping the public key weights and the target ciphertext into a geometric lattice, cryptanalysts transform the NP-complete subset sum problem into a Shortest Vector Problem (SVP) or Closest Vector Problem (CVP). When the knapsack system has low information density, the vector representing the original plaintext is unusually short compared to random lattice vectors, allowing the polynomial-time LLL algorithm to discover it and decrypt the secret message directly.

The Mechanics of Knapsack Cryptosystems

In an additive knapsack cryptosystem, such as the Merkle-Hellman scheme, the sender encrypts a binary plaintext vector \(\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) \in \{0, 1\}^n\) using a public sequence of integers (weights) \(\mathbf{a} = (a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n)\). The ciphertext \(C\) is computed as the dot product:

\[C = \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i a_i\]

Decryption for an unauthorized party requires solving the subset sum problem: finding a binary vector \(\mathbf{x}\) that sums to \(C\) using the public weights. While the general subset sum problem is NP-complete, the specific instances generated by knapsack cryptosystems contain structural vulnerabilities related to their density.

The density \(d\) of a knapsack system is defined as:

\[d = \frac{n}{\max_i(\log_2 a_i)}\]

To prevent spurious solutions (accidental modular collisions that lead to identical ciphertexts), practical implementations require large public weights, which inherently lowers the density of the knapsack (\(d < 1\)). This low density is the primary flaw exploited by lattice reduction attacks.

Constructing the Lattice

Cryptanalysts formulate the knapsack problem as a lattice using basis matrices originally developed by researchers such as Lagarias, Odlyzko, and Coster. A common representation is an \((n+1) \times (n+1)\) integer matrix \(B\), where each row forms a basis vector:

\[B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & \dots & 0 & a_1 \\ 0 & 1 & \dots & 0 & a_2 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & 1 & a_n \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & 0 & -C \end{pmatrix}\]

Any linear combination of these basis vectors using integer coefficients corresponds to a vector in the lattice \(\mathcal{L}(B)\). If we take the specific linear combination where the first \(n\) coefficients are the binary message bits \(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\) and the final coefficient is \(1\), the resulting lattice vector \(\mathbf{v}\) evaluates to:

\[\mathbf{v} = \left( x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n, \sum_{i=1}^n x_i a_i - C \right)\]

Because \(\sum x_i a_i = C\) by definition of the ciphertext, the final coordinate cancels out to zero:

\[\mathbf{v} = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n, 0)\]

Applying the LLL Algorithm

The Euclidean norm of this target vector \(\mathbf{v}\) is \(\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{\sum x_i^2} \le \sqrt{n}\).

In a high-dimensional lattice generated from large public weights \(a_i\), Minkowski's theorem dictates that a typical non-zero lattice vector will have a length roughly proportional to \(\det(\mathcal{L})^{1/(n+1)} \approx C^{1/(n+1)}\). When the knapsack density is sufficiently low, the public weights \(a_i\) (and therefore \(C\)) are exponentially larger than \(2^n\). As a result, the target vector \(\mathbf{v}\) with length \(\le \sqrt{n}\) is drastically shorter than almost every other vector in the lattice.

Finding the exact shortest vector in an arbitrary lattice is NP-hard, but the LLL algorithm approximates the shortest vector in polynomial time by generating an LLL-reduced basis satisfying:

  1. Size reduction: Projection coefficients between vectors are bounded by \(| \mu_{i,j} | \le \frac{1}{2}\).
  2. Lovász condition: Vectors are ordered such that no significant length reduction can be achieved by swapping adjacent Gram-Schmidt orthogonalized vectors.

When LLL runs on the constructed basis matrix \(B\), it progressively rotates and reduces the basis vectors toward their shortest, most orthogonal components. Because the target vector \((x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n, 0)\) is an extreme geometric outlier, LLL frequently places it directly into the reduced basis.

Density Thresholds and Cryptographic Impact

The effectiveness of LLL-based attacks depends heavily on the knapsack density:

  • Lagarias-Odlyzko Attack: Proved that knapsack cryptosystems with density \(d < 0.6463\) can be broken almost always in polynomial time using standard LLL reduction.
  • Coster et al. Refinements: By centering the binary variables using the transformation \(y_i = x_i - \frac{1}{2}\) to reduce the expected Euclidean norm to \(\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{n}\), the solvable density bound increased to \(d < 0.9408\).

Because avoiding spurious decryptions requires high public weights (leading to \(d < 1\)), virtually all standard single-iteration knapsack cryptosystems fall within the threshold solvable by LLL or its block-reduction variant, BKZ (Block Korkine-Zolotarev). Consequently, mathematical cryptanalysis through lattice reduction rendered early public-key knapsack schemes obsolete for secure communication.

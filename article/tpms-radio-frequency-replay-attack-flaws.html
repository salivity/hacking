TPMS Radio Frequency Replay Attack Flaws

Tire Pressure Monitoring Systems (TPMS) are vulnerable to signal replay attacks due to fundamental security omissions in their wireless radio frequency (RF) design. Because these embedded systems prioritize battery longevity and low manufacturing costs over data security, they routinely broadcast unencrypted, unauthenticated telemetry across public sub-gigahertz bands. Consequently, threat actors equipped with low-cost software-defined radios (SDRs) can intercept, store, and retransmit valid sensor frames to trick a vehicle's Electronic Control Unit (ECU) into accepting spoofed or outdated tire conditions.

Unencrypted Telemetry Broadcasts

TPMS sensors communicate with the vehicle's internal receiver using standard sub-gigahertz frequencies, typically 315 MHz in North America and 433 MHz in Europe and Asia. These transmissions utilize basic modulation schemes, such as Amplitude-Shift Keying (ASK) or Frequency-Shift Keying (FSK), with simple Manchester encoding. The primary flaw at the RF physical and data link layers is the total absence of cryptographic encryption. Sensor telemetry—including internal tire pressure, temperature, battery status, and sensor operational flags—is broadcast as plaintext, allowing any standard radio receiver tuned to the correct frequency to demodulate and record the raw bitstream without overcoming any cryptographic barrier.

Absence of Rolling Codes and Monotonic Counters

The most critical vulnerability permitting replay attacks is the lack of temporal freshness mechanisms. Unlike modern remote keyless entry (RKE) systems, which rely on rolling codes or cryptographic counters to invalidate previous transmissions, TPMS protocols generally omit nonces, timestamps, and sequence numbers. Because the packet payload does not change to reflect sequence progression, a frame recorded at one moment in time remains mathematically and logically valid indefinitely. When an attacker retransmits a recorded frame hours or days later, the ECU's receiver accepts it as an authentic, current transmission.

Static Sensor Identifiers

Every TPMS wheel sensor is assigned a static, factory-programmed identifier—typically 28 to 32 bits in length—to distinguish its data from neighboring wheels and nearby vehicles. The vehicle’s ECU evaluates incoming RF packets by validating whether the embedded sensor ID matches the list of authorized IDs paired with the vehicle. Because these IDs never change, an adversary who captures a single legitimate transmission from a target tire automatically acquires the required identifier. When the signal is replayed, the vehicle processes the data natively because the packet's ID matches the ECU's whitelist.

Lack of Cryptographic Message Authentication Codes

TPMS packets employ rudimentary integrity checks, universally relying on basic 8-bit or 16-bit Cyclic Redundancy Checks (CRCs). While a CRC is effective at detecting natural RF transmission errors and bit flips caused by environmental interference, it offers zero cryptographic protection. A CRC is not a Message Authentication Code (MAC); it uses a publicly known, deterministic mathematical algorithm. An attacker can modify any field within the captured payload—such as manually altering the pressure reading to simulate a blowout or flat tire—recalculate the valid CRC, and transmit the manipulated payload. The receiving ECU will accept the frame as authentic because the CRC matches the payload contents.

Asymmetric Transceiver Constraints

TPMS security is further hindered by the asymmetric design of the communication architecture. Sensors mounted directly inside the valve stem or wheel rim are passive emitters designed to operate autonomously for 5 to 10 years on a non-rechargeable coin-cell battery. Adding cryptographic handshakes, two-way challenge-response protocols via Low Frequency (125 kHz) wake-up channels, or heavy symmetric-key generation algorithms would dramatically increase power consumption. As a result, sensors transmit unidirectionally on simple intervals without verifying if the ECU actually received the packet or verifying the identity of the listening receiver, making the protocol inherently susceptible to signal capture and unauthorized replay.
